**If you’re reading this from Menorca around December 10th, congratulations.** You’ve reached the “sweet spot” of winter: the Constitution holiday is over, the official lighting ceremonies have taken place, and from now on, the island enters a phase of calm, local shopping, and gastronomic preparations.

As a guide from **Voyager Menorca**, my goal isn’t to give you a generic list, but to accompany you from town to town and explain those “Menorcan things” that often go unnoticed by visitors, but are the heart of our Christmas: card games that are only played these weeks, sweets that hardly leave the island, medieval rituals, and, recently, even a *Talayotic solstice* that has surprised the scientific community.

## 1. Route through towns: what to do from today

In December, life in Menorca changes pace. You won’t find the summer crowds, but you will find illuminated squares, markets full of local produce, and bars where families gather to play cards and discuss holiday preparations. If you want to experience an authentic winter, I suggest a small route through the main towns.

### **Ciutadella and Ferreries: the sound of cards and “Es Cuc”**

In the western area (Ciutadella and Ferreries), winter has a very specific sound: that of **cards hitting the table** and the voices of several generations sharing a game.

**El Joc des Cuc (or Sa Cuca)** is not a physical or agility game, but a **traditional card game** deeply rooted in the Christmas calendar. It is played by drawing concentric circles on a piece of paper; each participant advances along the “path” according to the cards they draw, with small domestic rules that vary from house to house. It is only played at this time of year, which makes it as much a family ritual as an entertainment.

If someone invites you to “fer un Cuc” at their house, accept without hesitation: it is one of the most local experiences you can have in Menorca in December. Don’t expect a tourist show, but an afternoon of cards, comments about the weather, and trays of pastries on the table.

Before or after the game, it is worth visiting the **Fira de Nadal de Ciutadella**, usually located around the Plaça de la Catedral and the old town. Among stately palaces and medieval streets, you will find craft stalls, local products, cheeses, sausages, and typical sweets. If you look closely, many exhibitors display seals such as *Menorca Reserva de Biosfera*, which identify projects aligned with the island’s sustainability.

Very close by, in **Ferreries**, the atmosphere is more intimate, but just as authentic. It is a good place to buy *avarques* made on the island (the traditional Menorcan sandals) and, if you are lucky, find a pastry shop that still makes **amargs** and other almond sweets in an artisanal way.

### **Maó: the legend of the Llumets and the Claustre del Carme**

In the capital, **Maó**, the Christmas imagery revolves around a very special legend: that of **Els Llumets**. According to oral tradition, they are four small goblins who live all year round on the islet of en Colom, within the enormous natural harbor of Maó. When December arrives, they cross the port, climb the bell tower of the church of Santa Maria, and turn on the city lights.

Although the official lighting ceremony has already taken place, strolling through the streets of the historic center at dusk allows you to imagine that magical climb to the bell tower. Notice the balconies, the 18th-century British facades, and the small shops that bring out boxes of nougat, panets, and bottles of sweet wine.

A must-see is the **Claustre del Carme**, an old convent converted into a market. In December it is in full swing: stalls of D.O. Maó-Menorca cheese, carn i xua, sobrasada, local wines, and small stalls where you will find **cuscussó**, almond tortadas, and other typical holiday sweets. It is the ideal place to fill your backpack with flavors of the island before returning home. You have the whole city in our [Maó guide](/en/visita-guiada-mahon-menorca/).

### **Es Mercadal: the sanctuary of sweets**

Located in the geographic center of Menorca, **Es Mercadal** becomes in winter an authentic **kilometer zero of Menorcan pastry**. Here are some of the workshops and pastry shops with the most history on the island, heirs to recipes passed down from generation to generation.

In the windows you will see names that may not yet sound familiar to you, but that for a Menorcan mean Christmas: **amargs** (almond and egg white cookies, intense and aromatic), **Torró de la Reina**, tortadas, carquinyols, pastissets… It is no coincidence that traditional pastry projects from Es Mercadal have become authentic references for those looking for Christmas sweets “from a lifetime”.

If you are looking for a panoramic view of the Menorcan winter, from Es Mercadal you can climb **Monte Toro**, the highest point on the island. On clear December days, the island is green, with the newly sown fields and the dry stone walls drawing a perfect mosaic.

### **Alaior: culture, warmth… and gateway to the Talayotic world**

**Alaior** stands out for its cultural life throughout the year: exhibitions, small theater programming, choral concerts, and a network of bars and cafes frequented by local people. Its old town, around the church of Santa Eulàlia, is ideal for an afternoon stroll, entering and leaving bakeries where the *pastissets* look like snow in the shape of a flower due to the layer of icing sugar.

In addition, the municipality of Alaior gives access to several Talayotic sites, including one that in recent years has become the protagonist of a surprising discovery: **So na Caçana**, which we will talk about below.

## 2. The Menorcan table: dishes with history

The Menorcan winter cannot be understood without the cuisine. The island has been incorporating ingredients and techniques from all the cultures that have passed through here: Roman, Arab, British, Catalan… At Christmas, that mixture is condensed into a few dishes that appear again and again on family tables and in the special menus of restaurants.

### **Menorcan-style stuffed squid: the star dish**

In Ciutadella and much of the island, **stuffed squid** are known as **“calamars plens”**. They are one of the central dishes of Christmas gastronomy: they appear at Christmas Eve dinners, Christmas lunches, New Year’s Eve and even New Year’s Day. Many traditional cuisine restaurants include them these days, and an increasing number offer menus to take home in which squid play a leading role.

The basis of the recipe is simple, but each house has its nuances. The squid is stuffed with its own legs and fins, finely chopped and sautéed with plenty of onion. From there, some families add breadcrumbs soaked in milk, hard-boiled egg, garlic and parsley; others also incorporate some minced meat, and in certain versions raisins and pine nuts are added, a heritage shared with Mallorca and other areas of the Mediterranean.

The secret is in the **slow cooking in sauce**: a base of onion, tomato, white wine and, often, a final picada of almonds that gives it density and a deep flavor, ideal for dipping bread until the plate is clean.

### **Cuscussó: the Arab legacy in the form of dessert**

If there is a sweet that surprises the visitor, that is the **cuscussó**. The name comes from the Arabic *kuskusú*, but in Menorca, over the centuries, it was transformed into a holiday dessert associated above all with Christmas.

Unlike North African couscous, here **grated peasant bread** is used, mixed with sugar, honey, ground almond, cinnamon, lemon zest and, according to the recipe, some lard. It is baked slowly until a compact, moist and very aromatic texture is achieved, which is served in small portions because it is substantial.

More than a sweet “for display”, cuscussó is a symbol of **resourceful cuisine** and the cultural mix of Menorca: hardened bread that is converted into a celebration dessert, with ingredients that tell us about the Arab past (honey, nuts, spices) and the European taste for sugar.

## 3. The Talayotic solstice of So na Caçana

Until very recently, winter in Menorca was associated almost exclusively with Christian holidays. However, a recent study has shown that more than two thousand years ago, in the middle of the **Talayotic era**, the island’s communities already celebrated the winter solstice in a very special way.

The site of **So na Caçana**, in the municipality of Alaior, preserves several Talayotic buildings, including a **taula enclosure** (sanctuary) oriented in a very precise way. Research has documented that, around the **winter solstice**, the sun’s rays enter through a small window open in the facade of the western sanctuary and travel inside to illuminate the area located in front of the central pillar, where it is believed that representations of divinities were placed.

This phenomenon is known as **“solar hierophany”**: a manifestation of the sacred through light. To verify this, the researchers carried out current measurements, recreations using 3D photogrammetry and calculations on the position of the sun in the Talayotic era. The results indicate that the light effect occurred even then, marking key dates within the ritual calendar of those communities.

Taula enclosure in So na Caçana (Alaior), with the central sacred space where the phenomenon of light is observed at the solstice. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

Every December, the **Agència Menorca Talaiòtica** and entities specialized in archeology organize guided tours to observe this phenomenon from inside the sanctuary. During the activity, the original darkness of the building is recreated and, when the sun reaches the appropriate height, a ray of light enters through the small window and travels through the space until it touches the sacred area. It is an impressive way to connect the current Christmas with the solar cycles that the Talayotics already celebrated.

If you want to experience it, I recommend keeping an eye on the activities section of **Menorca Talaiòtica** and the cultural agenda of the Consell Insular: places are limited and there is usually a draw or prior registration for the days around December 21. And if you are interested in the Talayotic world, you have our [complete guide to Talayotic Menorca](/en/menorca-talayotica-guia/).

## 4. Ses gloses: Christmas in improvised verse

In addition to liturgy and gastronomy, the Menorcan winter is also experienced in the form of **improvised word**. The **gloses** are sung stanzas, with rhyme and fixed meter, that the *glosadors* chain together on diverse themes: from local politics to daily life, through family jokes.

When the word “glosada” appears on the poster of a bar, casal or cultural center, it is worth entering. Normally you will see two or more glosadors in front of the public, accompanied by guitar or guitarrón, throwing verses at each other. At Christmas, many glosas review the year that ends with a tone between critical and affectionate, mixing humor and emotion.

If you don’t understand all the Menorcan Catalan, don’t worry: the energy of the room, the untimely laughter and the cadence of the voices are in themselves a cultural experience.

## 5. Where to buy sweets and try winter cuisine

Once you know what dishes and traditions to look for, the key question arrives: **where do I find them?** Here are some ideas to guide you, always from a local perspective and supporting projects that keep the gastronomic tradition of Menorca alive.

### **Es Mercadal: amargs, torró de la Reina and more**

In Es Mercadal there are workshops with a long history in Menorcan pastry. Reference pastry shops have been making ensaimadas, pastissets, carquinyols and the famous **amargs** for decades, using recipes passed down from generation to generation. From that same tradition arise projects that today are associated with the *Marca Menorca Reserva de Biosfera*, precisely because of their commitment to craftsmanship and local products.

If you want to make a truly Menorcan gastronomic gift, an assortment of amargs and almond tortada bought in Es Mercadal is a safe bet.

### **Markets in Maó and Ciutadella: local produce for your holiday menu**

To fill the Christmas basket with fresh produce, the covered markets are your allies. In Maó, the **Mercat del Claustre del Carme** brings together stalls of cheeses with D.O. Maó-Menorca, traditional sausages, bakeries that prepare *panets* and seasonal sweets, as well as small displays of prepared dishes where, with luck, you will find **calamars plens** or fish stews to take away.

In Ciutadella, the **Mercat des Peix** and its surroundings are perfect for getting fresh squid fished in the light of the boats in the bay, ideal if your accommodation has a kitchen and you want to try your own version of stuffed squid.

### **Restaurants to try stuffed squid and Menorcan cuisine**

At the restaurant level, many **Menorcan cuisine restaurants** in Ciutadella, Maó and Es Mercadal include special menus with winter dishes at this time: bullit de carne, roasts, cannelloni and, of course, **calamars plens**. Not all places offer it daily, so it is advisable to ask specifically for this dish when booking. You have our favorite addresses in the [Menorca restaurant guide 2026](/en/restaurantes-menorca-2026/).

If you are looking for more creative versions, some gastronomic restaurants have reinterpreted the classic recipe incorporating local products and current techniques. The key is always the same: ask for seasonal Menorcan cuisine options and see what the chef proposes for those dates.
