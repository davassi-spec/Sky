On **Wednesday, August 12, 2026**, at sunset, Menorca will experience one of the most extraordinary astronomical spectacles seen in Europe in living memory: a **total solar eclipse**. It will be the first total solar eclipse visible from the Iberian Peninsula in over a century, and it won’t happen again from our island for generations. Using official data from the **National Geographic Institute (IGN)** and the **Government of the Balearic Islands (Govern de les Illes Balears)**, this guide brings together everything you need: what an eclipse is and how it works, exact timings down to the second, maps and the best places to watch it, the **official observation zones (ZOO)** and access arrangements, weather, eye safety, photography, mobility, tips for families, children, older people and people with reduced mobility, the most common mistakes, and a complete plan for the day. No rush, no fine print.

## What exactly is a total solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon positions itself exactly between the Earth and the Sun, casting its shadow onto the Earth’s surface. It might seem like this should happen every month, with every new moon, but it doesn’t: the Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5 degrees relative to the Earth’s orbit around the Sun, so the lunar shadow almost always passes “above” or “below” the Earth. The three celestial bodies only align with the necessary precision a couple of times a year, and of those occasions, very few produce a total eclipse visible from an inhabited location.

The Moon’s shadow has two parts: a central, dark area called the **umbra**, where the Sun is completely covered, and a wider, fainter outer area called the **penumbra**, where only part of the solar disk is obscured. If you are within the umbra—a narrow band barely a hundred kilometers wide that sweeps across the Earth at high speed—you see a **total eclipse**. If you are in the penumbra, you see a **partial eclipse**, much more common and far less spectacular. Menorca is fortunate to be right within that narrow band of totality on August 12, 2026.

During the seconds when the Sun is completely hidden, its **corona** becomes visible: the faint outer atmosphere of the Sun, normally invisible due to the glare of the solar disk, which appears as a whitish, diffuse halo around the black silhouette of the Moon. It is, according to those who have witnessed it, one of nature’s most awe-inspiring sights.

### Total, partial and annular eclipses: quick differences

| Type | What happens | Can you look without glasses? |
|---|---|---|
| **Total** | The Moon covers 100% of the solar disk; the corona appears | Only during exact totality |
| **Partial** | The Moon covers only part of the Sun | Never without protection |
| **Annular** | The Moon passes in front but leaves a “ring of fire” | Never without protection |

Menorca’s 2026 eclipse is **total**. It’s the only type that allows you, for a few brief seconds, to look at the Sun safely with the naked eye.

## Why this eclipse is so special for Spain and Menorca

The last total solar eclipse visible from Spain took place on **April 17, 1912**; in the Balearic Islands, the last one was on **August 30, 1905**. In any case, more than a century has passed. The 2026 eclipse will be the first total eclipse visible from the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands in over a hundred years. This will be followed by another total eclipse on **August 2, 2027** (visible as total only from the southern tip of Andalusia, Ceuta, and Melilla) and an **annular** eclipse on **January 26, 2028**, completing the so-called “Iberian eclipse trio.” After the total eclipse of August 2, 2027, Spain will not experience another total solar eclipse until **2053**; the 2028 one will be annular, not total. It is, without exaggeration, a “once-in-a-lifetime” opportunity for the vast majority of us.

What makes the 2026 eclipse unique in our islands is that it occurs practically at sunset. The path of totality travels across the Earth from west to east—from the Arctic, through Greenland and Iceland, crossing the Iberian Peninsula from A Coruña to the Balearics—and reaches Menorca in its final stages, with the Sun already very low on the horizon. Menorca will be the first Balearic island to enter totality and one of the last Spanish territories crossed by the lunar shadow, with the Sun between 1.4° and 2° high depending on the point on the island: the choice of observation site is absolutely decisive, as we will see.

## Eclipse Timings in Menorca

These are the times calculated for the **municipality of Ciutadella de Menorca** (official peninsular and Balearic time, CEST), according to the National Geographic Institute’s viewer. They may vary by a few seconds depending on the exact point on the island from which you observe:

| Phase | Time (Ciutadella, IGN) | Sun Altitude |
|---|---|---|
| Start of the partial eclipse (first contact) | **7:37:16 PM** | ≈ 12° |
| Start of totality (second contact) | **8:30:16 PM** | ≈ 2° |
| Maximum eclipse | **8:30:51 PM** | ≈ 2° |
| End of totality (third contact) | **8:31:27 PM** | ≈ 2° |
| Sunset | **8:45:28 PM** | 0° |
| End of observation from Menorca | At sunset: the partial phase continues afterwards, but already below the horizon | — |

**Duration of totality in Ciutadella: approximately 1 minute and 11 seconds** (IGN data for the municipality; varies by a few seconds depending on the exact point). According to data from the Government of the Balearic Islands, totality begins first in Menorca and progresses south to Formentera (where the first point enters totality around **8:32:49 PM**). It will be slightly shorter than in Mallorca (where it reaches one and a half minutes) but an equally unforgettable moment. Sunset occurs around 8:45:28 PM in Ciutadella; the partial phase astronomically continues after sunset, but is no longer visible from the island. To check the exact time — to the second — at your specific location, the most reliable tool is the official IGN viewer, which allows you to enter any point on the map.

## Day schedule, minute by minute

An eclipse afternoon isn’t just the minute of totality: it’s an experience that unfolds over more than an hour. Here’s the approximate script for Menorca’s west coast:

| Time | What happens and what to do |
|---|---|
| **Until 5:00 PM** | Arrive at your observation point. In the official zones of Ciutadella, traffic closes from that time (earlier if there are large crowds). Park, set up your chair, locate west. |
| **5:00 PM–7:30 PM** | Active waiting: picnic, check eclipse glasses, prepare camera and tripod. |
| **7:37:16 PM** | First contact. The Moon begins to “bite” the Sun. From here on, keep your glasses on whenever you look at the Sun. |
| **7:37 PM–8:30 PM** | Growing partial phase. The light turns strange, metallic; the temperature drops; the sea changes color. |
| **Moments before totality** | Baily’s beads and the diamond ring appear. Keep your glasses on until the last bright point of the Sun disappears. |
| **8:30:16 PM–8:31:27 PM** | **TOTALITY.** When the Sun is completely covered, remove your glasses and look at the corona with the naked eye. Just over a minute you won’t forget. |
| **8:31:27 PM** | The first flash reappears: glasses back on immediately. |
| **8:45:28 PM** | The Sun sets still partially eclipsed over the sea. End of observation from Menorca… and the beginning of the Perseids night. |

## Duration and Sun altitude: why Menorca is an extreme case

In most eclipses, the recommendation is to seek height. Here it is the opposite. The fact that changes everything is the height of the Sun: **during totality it will be approximately between 1.4° and 2° above the horizon, depending on the exact observation point in Menorca**—the lowest in all of Spain; in Ciutadella it is around 2°. For comparison, in A Coruña the Sun will be at 12 degrees and in Burgos at 8. Two degrees is, roughly, the width of two fingers held together at arm’s length.

This has three practical consequences:

- Any obstacle—a hill, a building, a tree, a boat, even a bank of low clouds—can completely block totality.
- The solar corona, which can be up to 3 degrees across, will sit almost “resting” on the sea: a rare and spectacular image.
- The sea horizon on the west coast is, literally, the best possible “seat”, because the sea guarantees a clean, unobstructed line.

## The best places to watch the eclipse in Menorca

Since the Sun will be almost touching the horizon, the key is not height but **a completely open and unobstructed western horizon**: no mountains, buildings, groves, or low clouds. All recommended points are in the western half of the island. I’ve grouped them by type for you to choose according to your plan, but keep in mind the [official access arrangements](#afluencia): several of them are regulated on August 12.

### Best beaches and coves to watch it

- **Cala en Bosc (official observation zone):** coast open to the sunset, with a promenade by the sea, bars, and restrooms nearby. The ZOO covers from the promenade car park to the Cap d’Artrutx lighthouse. Parking is ample but will fill up very quickly that day, and there’s little shade left by that time. Traffic will be closed from 5:00 PM, or earlier if crowd levels demand it.
- **Cala Blanca (official observation zone):** somewhat less crowded, with an open western horizon from the side rocks of the cove and restaurants in the urbanization itself. The ZOO runs from Clot de sa Cera to Es Mirador restaurant. Street parking is available; it’s advisable to arrive early and walk the last few meters.
- **Cala Santandria:** a wind-sheltered cove a few minutes from Ciutadella, comfortable for families, with bars by the sand. It is not an official zone, so there will be no specific arrangements: street parking is limited, and the earlier you arrive, the better your spot.
- **Son Xoriguer and the Cap d’Artrutx area:** the southwestern tip of the island, with the Artrutx lighthouse as a reference, a flat promenade facing the sunset directly, and Cala en Bosc services a short walk away. There is usually a sea breeze: pleasant in August, but secure your tripod.

### Best lighthouses

- **Punta Nati Lighthouse (regulated access on August 12):** offers an excellent western horizon, but the official arrangements **close the car park from 8:00 AM** and regulate access via the CF-5 road. Ciutadella also considers it a **risk zone**, along with Cala Morell and Pont d’en Gil, with Local Police control. Do not expect to be able to arrive by car that day. Outside of August 12, it’s still wonderful: it’s on the [Camí de Cavalls](/en/tourist-guide-cami-de-cavalls/) and is one of the darkest areas on the island.
- **Artrutx Lighthouse:** at the southwest vertex, with perfect sunset orientation, flat terrain, and relatively close parking. It is within the official observation zone of Cala en Bosc: of the lighthouses, it is the most convenient and the only one with official arrangements.
- **Cavalleria Lighthouse (regulated access on August 12):** its cliffs open to the west offer a clear horizon and a spectacular setting, but the **Camí de Tramuntana to the lighthouse (CF-3) will be regulated** that day, as will the Camí de Tramuntana itself at the Me-15 / Me-7 intersection roundabout. The official alternative in that area of the island is Carrer des Tamarells, in Platges de Fornells.

You can read more in our [guide to Menorca’s 7 lighthouses](/en/guia-faros-menorca/).

### Best cliffs and viewpoints

- **Cliffs of Punta Nati and the north coast of Ciutadella:** height and a 180° sea horizon, but affected by the same access arrangements as the Punta Nati lighthouse.
- **Pont d’en Gil:** the natural arch on the west coast, one of the island’s most famous sunset viewpoints, but **considered a risk zone on August 12**, with police control. It can be reached on foot from the Cala en Blanes area via a stretch of coast with no shade or services and an open cliff: it’s not the place for a night of maximum attendance. Very close official alternative: **Avinguda Los Delfines**.
- **Passeig Marítim de Ciutadella:** flat, paved, and accessible, with clear views of the sea, the port, and the old town in the background, and bars and services nearby. It is not listed as an official zone, but it is one of the busiest on the island: reserve your spot well in advance. The nearest official zone is Son Blanc. See our guide to [9 sunset viewpoints in Menorca](/en/sunset-menorca-9-viewpoints/).

### From the sea

Observing the eclipse from the sea can offer a clear horizon, but it requires professional planning. It should only be done with an experienced skipper or an authorized company, after consulting the marine forecast, navigation warnings, and any restrictions that may be established. The Island Council also reminds you **not to anchor over Posidonia meadows**, maintain a safe distance between vessels, avoid risky maneuvers, and plan your return to port in advance, as a very high concentration of boats is expected right at the end of the eclipse.

### Archaeological sites

The Island Council will deploy wardens at **Castell de Santa Àgueda** and **Torre d’en Galmès** and other publicly owned sites, to manage access and prevent climbing on talayots, towers, and heritage structures. These are monuments over three thousand years old: they are to be looked at, not climbed.

## Sun direction and horizon

At maximum eclipse, the Sun will be towards the **west-northwest, with an azimuth of about 288°**. In practice: stand facing the sea on the west coast and look for the point where the Sun is setting. A compass (or the one in the Horitzó 2026 app) will confirm the exact direction. The day before, at the same time, check that there’s no obstacle (crane, mast, headland) on that line. The Govern carried out an official visibility rehearsal on April 29 and 30 precisely to verify these points: if the Sun was visible at that time on those days, it will also be visible on August 12.

## Weather: chances of clear skies

Climatologically, August is one of Menorca’s most stable and driest months, with predominantly clear skies and a low chance of rain. That’s in your favour. But an eclipse this low on the horizon has a specific enemy: **low clouds over the sea**, which can form even if the rest of the sky is clear. Recommendations:

- Check the **AEMET** forecast and cloud maps in the 3–4 days beforehand, paying attention to low cloud on the sea horizon.
- Always have a **plan B** at another point on the west coast that you can move to with enough time, considering the planned traffic restrictions.
- Remember that **high clouds** (cirrus) can still allow you to see the event; low clouds are what ruin it.
- From the sea there is more room to avoid cloud cover, but only with an experienced skipper or an authorized company (see the “From the sea” section).

## Light pollution and Menorca’s Starlight advantage

Menorca has been a **Starlight Reserve and Tourist Destination** since 2019 (certified by the Starlight Foundation), and the whole island is part of a Biosphere Reserve. It has a Night Sky Protection Regulation and **maximum darkness zones**—Punta Nati, Macarella-Son Saura, Cavalleria-Cala del Pilar, Monte Toro, s’Albufera des Grau, among others—with virtually no light pollution.

For the eclipse itself, with the Sun still above the horizon, light pollution is not a decisive factor. But it becomes crucial right after: if you stay at a dark spot on the west coast after sunset, you will have an exceptional sky for the second part of the night (see Perseids, below). Keep in mind that several of these areas have regulated access on August 12.

## Access restrictions, attendance, and mobility: the official arrangements

A very high influx of “eclipse hunters” and visitors from all over Europe is expected, in the middle of August and with the island already in high season. The Balearic Government activated the **ECLIPBAL** protocol, within the Territorial Civil Protection Plan, and the security, mobility, and forest protection arrangements have already been published, in coordination with the Island Council and the municipalities. These are the points that most affect you as a visitor.

### General restrictions

- In the **four official observation zones of Ciutadella**, traffic will be closed **from 5:00 PM**, or earlier if crowd levels demand it.
- In the **rest of Menorca**, schedules and restrictions depend on each road and municipality. Generally, restrictions on affected roads will apply **between 3:00 PM and 9:00 PM**, although they may be brought forward or extended for safety reasons.
- The restrictions **do not affect** residents, essential services, public transport, or duly justified displacements.

### Regulated or closed accesses in Menorca

- **Punta Nati Lighthouse (CF-5):** regulated access and **car park closed from 8:00 AM**.
- **Camí de Tramuntana:** regulation at the Me-15 / Me-7 intersection roundabout.
- **Cavalleria Lighthouse (CF-3):** Camí de Tramuntana to the lighthouse, regulated.
- **Monte Toro (Me-13):** road from Es Mercadal to El Toro, regulated.
- **Ferreries:** closure of access roads to l’Enclusa and Camí de Tramuntana d’Ets Alocs, with gradual closures (first partial on the Me-1, then at the access to the Binissuès estate and, finally, access only for residents of the Camí de Tramuntana estates and tourist accommodations).
- **Sant Lluís:** Passeig de Cap d’en Font closed at both intersections with Carrer del Caló Blanc, and Passeig de la Mar (Binissafúller Roters) at the crossings with Llevant and Tramuntana, **between 7:00 PM and 9:30 PM**.
- **Maó:** possible closure of Passeig Marítim from 7:00 PM and restrictions in Binidalí from 6:30 PM; controls in Es Canutells, Es Grau, and other high-traffic points; restricted access to Favàritx and closure of the batteries during the day.
- **Ciutadella:** **Cala Morell, Punta Nati Lighthouse, and Pont d’en Gil** are considered risk zones, with Local Police control.

### Screens to follow the eclipse without traveling

If you prefer to avoid roads and crowds, several municipalities will install screens: **Ciutadella**, in Plaça des Born; **Alaior**, in Sa Plaça; and **Maó**, at Cinemes Ocimax and in the port.

### Parking and shuttle bus

For the **Son Bou** area, it is recommended to access via **Torre Solí**, where a car park will be set up in Torre Solí II with a **continuous shuttle bus between 5:00 PM and 12:30 AM**. This is probably the most convenient option on the entire island if you are not in a hurry and want to forget about the car.

**Practical consequence:** do not rely on a single access point or improvise at the last minute. Measures will be updated in the days leading up to the event based on expected crowd levels, so **check [the official Government information](https://www.caib.es/webgoib/es/-/saber-ne-m%C3%A9s-preguntes-m%C3%A9s-freq%C3%BCents) on the day itself**, and always follow the instructions of emergency services.

## How to get there, parking and public transport

### How to get to the west coast

Most recommended spots are in the municipality of Ciutadella, at the western end of the island, about 45 minutes by car from Maó airport via the main road Me-1. From Ciutadella, the southwest resorts (Cala en Bosc, Cala Blanca, Cala Santandria) are 10–15 minutes away. That day, allow much more time than usual.

### Parking

- In Ciutadella there is free parking on the Sant Nicolau promenade and the seafront, as well as paid parking (blue zone) in the centre.
- If you go by car to one of the official zones in Ciutadella, **arrive before 5:00 PM**, because traffic may be closed even earlier depending on crowd levels. Whenever possible, walk from your accommodation or use public transport.
- In natural areas, space is limited and several accesses are regulated: never park outside permitted areas or block roads or emergency accesses.

### Public transport

Menorca has regular bus lines connecting Maó and Ciutadella year-round, and in summer there are services from Ciutadella station to the southern and southwestern coves. That day demand will be higher than usual and some lines may be reinforced or reorganized: check updated timetables with the operator. Taking public transport—or using the Torre Solí shuttle bus for Son Bou—and walking the last stretch can save you the parking hassle.

## Eye Safety: The Most Important Thing

You should never look directly at the Sun without adequate protection, **except when the solar disk is completely covered by the Moon during totality**.

![Infographic in Spanish on how to safely observe a solar eclipse with eye protection](/images/infografia-seguridad-eclipse-eso.jpg)

How to safely observe the eclipse with eye protection. Credit: ESO / B. Tafreshi (twanight.org) / P. Horálek.

- Always use **certified eclipse glasses** (standard **ISO 12312-2**) whenever you look directly at the Sun during the partial phases. Regular sunglasses, no matter how dark they seem, do not protect: they allow infrared and ultraviolet radiation to pass through, which can damage the retina without you noticing. X-rays, smoked glass, or homemade filters are also not suitable.
- Buy your glasses in advance from a reliable supplier: they sell out in the days beforehand. The Govern has reminded the retail sector that only products that meet international standards should be sold.
- **Only remove your glasses when the Sun is completely covered and no bright part is visible. Put them back on immediately when the first flash of light reappears.** The times are only indicative: be guided by what you see, not by the clock.
- If you use a camera, binoculars or a telescope, they need their **own solar filter** during the partial phase; looking at the Sun through optics without a filter is especially dangerous.
- An alternative and completely safe method is **indirect projection**: make a small hole in a cardboard sheet and project the Sun’s image onto another surface, never looking directly.

## Eclipse photography

Photographing an eclipse with the Sun at 2° above the sea is difficult and beautiful at the same time. Ideas to get it right:

- **Partial phase:** a solar filter on the lens is mandatory. Without it, you’ll damage the sensor (and your eyesight if you use an optical viewfinder).
- **Totality:** when the Sun is completely covered, remove the filter to capture the corona. Use varied exposures (bracketing) because the brightness range is enormous. Put the filter back on as soon as the first flash reappears.
- **With a phone:** solar filter in front of the lens during the partial phase; during totality you can remove it. Lock focus and exposure manually.
- **Composition:** take advantage of how low the Sun is to include the sea, a lighthouse or a coastline silhouette. That’s Menorca’s big differentiator.
- **Golden tip:** dedicate most of that little more than a minute to **watching**, not to the screen. The live corona cannot be captured in any photo.

## Tips for families, children, older people and reduced mobility

### For families and children

- It’s a fantastic educational experience: explain beforehand how the Sun–Earth–Moon system works using a torch and two balls.
- **The Government recommends that children under six years of age do not directly observe the eclipse.** For them, indirect projection, a screen, or television broadcast is advised.
- Other minors must be **constantly supervised by an adult**, with their own certified glasses throughout the partial phase.
- Choose a comfortable spot with nearby services (Cala en Bosc, Cala Blanca, Son Bou with shuttle bus) to have restrooms and shade during the long wait.
- Turn the afternoon into a picnic: it’ll make the wait easier and create the memory.

### For older people

- Prioritize points with easy access and little walking: Son Blanc, Avinguda Los Delfines, or Cala Santandria.
- A chair with a backrest, plenty of water and sun protection; the wait can be several hours in the heat.
- Avoid narrow road accesses and the walking sections of the lighthouses, which are also regulated that day.
- The screens in Plaça des Born (Ciutadella), Sa Plaça (Alaior), or Maó port are a very convenient alternative.

### For people with reduced mobility

- The flat and paved official zones of Ciutadella—**Son Blanc and Avinguda Los Delfines**—and the **Passeig Marítim de Ciutadella** are the best options: flat ground, western horizon, and services nearby.
- Some Ciutadella beaches have boardwalks; check which ones will keep access that day.
- Arrive well before 5:00 PM to secure an accessible parking space nearby, given the special traffic arrangements.
- Check [the official website](https://www.caib.es/webgoib/es/-/saber-ne-m%C3%A9s-preguntes-m%C3%A9s-freq%C3%BCents) to see if the observation zones provide reserved spaces.

## Mistakes most visitors make

- **Choosing the site for the landscape and not for the western horizon.** A beautiful viewpoint facing east or covered by a hill is not suitable for this eclipse.
- **Assuming an official zone is the best for viewing.** ZOOs were chosen for safety and capacity, not visibility. Check your spot with Horitzó 2026.
- **Arriving late.** In the official zones of Ciutadella, traffic closes from 5:00 PM and there are regulated roads: anyone leaving at the last minute may get stuck during totality.
- **Counting on arriving by car at Punta Nati or Cavalleria.** Both accesses are regulated on August 12 and Punta Nati car park closes at 8:00 AM.
- **Using regular sunglasses.** They don’t protect you. Only ISO 12312-2 certified glasses work.
- **Buying glasses on the day.** They sell out; buy them in advance.
- **Not checking the actual horizon.** A mast, a crane, or a bank of low clouds can block 2°.
- **Watching totality through your phone.** Experience it with your eyes; the camera doesn’t capture what the eye sees.
- **Forgetting that it’s at sunset.** Afterwards there’s no margin: the Sun sets. There will be no “second chance” that day.

## Historical curiosities: how previous eclipses were experienced in Spain

August 12, 2026 won’t be the first time the Balearics become a global stage for an eclipse. On **August 30, 1905**, a total solar eclipse crossed Spain and was visible as total from the islands. Unlike the 2026 eclipse, that one happened at **midday** (around 12:02 in the Balearics) and totality lasted almost **four minutes**, making it an extraordinary scientific opportunity.

It was a major social and scientific event. Dozens of international expeditions travelled to the mainland and the islands: British and German commissions and the Russian Academy of Sciences. In Burgos, hot-air balloons—the *Júpiter*, the *Urano* and the *Marte*—were launched, and the young engineer **Emilio Herrera** became one of the first people to observe totality from the air, leaving a memorable description of the corona above a sea of clouds. In Mallorca, expeditions set up at the Son Espanyolet Velodrome and the Grand Hotel; factories were even ordered to close during the eclipse so smoke wouldn’t cloud the atmosphere. One of the drivers of the British expedition, G. F. Chambers, ended up observing it **from the sea**—an option that, with proper professional planning, still makes sense 121 years later.

In Spain as a whole, the last total solar eclipse before 2026 was on **April 17, 1912**. Those early years of the 20th century formed a historic “trio” (1900, 1905, and 1912) that the international astronomical community dubbed “the Spanish eclipses.” History rhymes: 2026, 2027, and 2028 form the new Iberian trio.

## A Golden End to the Night: The Perseids

The eclipse date coincides almost exactly with the peak of the **Perseids**, the famous August meteor shower. If the sky stays clear and you’re at a dark spot on the west coast—one of the Starlight maximum-darkness zones—you can stay after sunset and enjoy a second astronomical show that same night: two celestial events in a single evening, something truly exceptional. Menorca, as a Starlight Reserve, is one of the best places in the Mediterranean for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What time is the eclipse in Menorca?

In Ciutadella, according to the IGN, the partial eclipse begins at **7:37:16 PM** and totality runs from **8:30:16 PM to 8:31:27 PM** (with the maximum at 8:30:51 PM). Sunset occurs at **8:45:28 PM**. Times may vary by a few seconds depending on the exact observation point.

### How long does totality last?

In Ciutadella, **approximately 1 minute and 11 seconds** (IGN data for the municipality). Menorca is the first point in the Balearic Islands where totality begins, with the Sun between 1.4° and 2° above the horizon depending on the location: the lowest altitude in all of Spain.

### What are the official observation zones in Menorca?

Nine: **Cala en Bosc, Cala Blanca, Avinguda Los Delfines, and Son Blanc** (Ciutadella), **Carrer des Tamarells** (Platges de Fornells), **Passeig Marítim de Maó**, and the beaches of **Son Bou, Sant Tomàs, and Talis**. In the four Ciutadella zones, traffic closes from 5:00 PM. The Government reminds that these are the safest and most accessible points, not necessarily those with the best visibility.

### Will it get dark in Menorca?

Not completely, since it happens very close to sunset. The sky will darken noticeably, with a twilight tone in 360°, and the temperature will drop, but it won’t be full night.

### Do I need to go to a special place or can I see it from anywhere in Menorca?

The entire island is within the path of totality, so technically it can be seen from anywhere. What makes the difference is having a **clear western horizon**, because the Sun will be very low. If you can see it from your accommodation with a western horizon, it is the most comfortable and safest option.

### What if it’s cloudy that day?

August usually brings stable skies in Menorca, but no one guarantees it. High clouds allow you to see part of the phenomenon; low clouds on the western horizon could obscure totality. Check the forecast in the days leading up to it, verify your spot with the Horitzó 2026 app, and have a Plan B.

### How do I protect my eyes?

With **ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses** whenever you look at the Sun during the partial phases. You can only remove them when the Sun is completely covered, and you must put them back on as soon as the first flash reappears. Sunglasses, X-rays, or smoked glass are not suitable.

### Can I take photos with my phone?

Yes, with a solar filter on the lens during the partial phase. You can remove it during totality. Manually lock focus and exposure and prepare your shot beforehand, because totality lasts little more than a minute.

### Is it safe for children?

The Government recommends that **children under six years of age do not directly observe** the eclipse: for them, indirect projection, a screen, or television broadcast. Other minors can observe it with certified glasses throughout the partial phase and under constant adult supervision. It is a magnificent educational experience.

### When was the last total eclipse visible in the Balearic Islands?

August 30, 1905. In Spain as a whole, the last total eclipse before 2026 was on **April 17, 1912**. After the total eclipse of August 2, 2027, there will be no other total eclipse in Spain until **2053**; the 2028 one will be **annular**, not total.

### Will there be traffic cuts or many people?

Yes. In the four official zones of Ciutadella, traffic closes **from 5:00 PM** (earlier if there are large crowds). In the rest of the island, restrictions depend on each road and municipality, generally **between 3:00 PM and 9:00 PM**. Access to Far de Punta Nati (CF-5, with parking closed from 8:00 AM), Camí de Tramuntana, Far de Cavalleria (CF-3), and Monte Toro (Me-13) are regulated, in addition to closures in Ferreries and Sant Lluís. Consult the details in the [official arrangements](#afluencia) section.
